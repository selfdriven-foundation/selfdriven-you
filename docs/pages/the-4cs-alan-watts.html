Alan Watts · 1915–1973

The watercourse way.

Alan Watts spent forty years explaining Zen, Taoism and Vedanta to Western audiences in plain, funny English. His central claim was simple: you are something the whole universe is doing, and most of your strain comes from forgetting that. Here he is, read through the four modes.

Who he was

A translator, not a teacher of rules.

Watts found Buddhism as a schoolboy in England, published his first book on Zen at twenty-one, and moved to the United States in 1938. He trained as an Episcopal priest, served as a university chaplain, then left the church in 1950 and settled in California.

From there he taught at the American Academy of Asian Studies, broadcast weekly on Berkeley's KPFA, and lectured wherever he was asked. He never claimed to be a Zen master. His skill was translation: taking ideas that sounded exotic and showing they describe ordinary experience.

Four modes

What Watts adds to each mode.

One idea from his work for each mode, with something small to do about it.

Mode 01 · Curious

Ask who is asking.

Watts thought the most interesting question was the one nobody is encouraged to ask: what do you mean by "I"? The usual answer is a small self sitting behind the eyes, steering a body. He argued that this is a social convention and a poor description of what is going on.

Look closely and the boundary blurs. You did not decide to beat your heart or grow your hair. The same process that does those things also does the weather and the tides.

Source: The Book (1966)

Try it

  • 2 minutesListen to the sounds around you without naming them. Notice that hearing happens with no effort from you.
  • TodayCatch one moment where you say "I" and ask what exactly it refers to.
  • This weekRead chapter one of The Book. It is short and funny.
Mode 02 · Caring

Nothing exists alone.

For Watts an organism and its environment were one process seen from two ends. A bee implies flowers. A person implies air, soil, language and other people. Separation is a useful way of talking and a misleading way of living.

Care follows from that without needing a moral lecture. If the other person is part of the field you are also part of, paying attention to them is accuracy as much as kindness.

Source: Nature, Man and Woman (1958)

Try it

  • 2 minutesPick one thing you used today and trace three people it depended on.
  • TodayIn one conversation, listen as if the other person were telling you something about yourself.
  • This weekThank someone whose work you rely on and never see.
Mode 03 · Constructive

Work the way you would dance.

Watts kept returning to music. Nobody plays a piece to reach the final chord, and nobody dances to arrive at a spot on the floor. He thought we treat life as a journey with a prize at the end and miss the point, which was the playing.

This is still a way of getting things done. Work held as play tends to be better work. The grain of the wood, the shape of the problem and the timing of the moment do part of the job when you stop forcing them.

Source: The Tao of Philosophy lectures; Tao: The Watercourse Way (1975)

Try it

  • 2 minutesDo one small task at half speed and give it your full attention.
  • TodayOn the stuck thing, ask which way it already wants to go, then push that way.
  • This weekMake something with no audience and no deadline.
Mode 04 · Chill

Stop stirring the water.

His best-known idea is the backwards law. Chase security and you feel more anxious. Grip the water and it runs through your fingers. Try hard to relax and you tense up. Some things only arrive when you stop reaching for them.

The Taoists called the alternative wu wei, action without forcing. Watts compared the mind to muddy water, which clears fastest when left alone. Rest, in this picture, is the condition for seeing straight.

Source: The Wisdom of Insecurity (1951)

Try it

  • 2 minutesSit and let your breath run itself. Do not improve it.
  • TodayLeave one worry unsolved on purpose until tomorrow.
  • This weekTake a walk with no destination and no headphones.

Read with care

He said he was not a guru. Believe him.

Watts described himself as a philosophical entertainer. He had no formal Zen training, and scholars have criticised how freely he simplified the traditions he drew on. He also drank heavily in his later years, and his own life did not always match the ease he described.

None of that cancels the ideas. It does suggest how to use them: as a way of looking to test against your own experience, with no one to follow.
